(a) only donors whose publication delta passed a secret scan with zero findings;
(b) only merges whose parents are ALREADY published (so the act combines published history, never

(c) merge commits only - no squash, no rebase, no force, no amend;
(d) one ledger line per push;
(e) the governance repo and the monorepo are always human-gated, permanently out of scope;
(f) post-push ls-remote must equal the local merge SHA; any divergence freezes the WHOLE queue;
(g) where a merge resolved a conflict, the merge commit is diffed against BOTH parents and that
    delta is secret-scanned before publication;
(h) donors published one at a time; first guard failure freezes the queue until a human releases it;
(i) scope and term bound; anything not listed is excluded by default.
Term: end of iteration 1. Closure: rotation of the factory credential afterwards.
Permanent exclusions: the governance repo (protected main, 149 unlanded commits held by another
session), a repo under quarantine (a self-generated private key exists in two local unpublished
branches), and the monorepo itself.

Relevant history: one donor's land hit an add/add conflict in two monitoring config files where
neither side was a superset; a naive "take one side" would have silently dropped either alerting
coverage or a metrics exporter. The resolution was a union, validated with dockerised
promtool/amtool against a pre-merge baseline control. Guard (g) exists because of that incident.

1. No. “Published parents only” bounds ancestry, not the merge commit’s tree. A merge commit can contain arbitrary content present in neither parent—through conflict resolution, generated files, manual edits, merge drivers, or deliberately constructed “evil merge” commits.

Guard (g) catches only secret-scanner-detectable additions in merges known to have resolved conflicts. It misses:

- Extra content inserted into an apparently clean merge.
- Non-secret harmful changes: disabled alerts, weakened controls, malicious workflows, backdoors, or configuration regressions.
- Secrets the scanner does not recognize.
- Dangerous deletions or substitutions.
- Submodule changes, LFS availability problems, and generated artifacts requiring separate validation.
- Semantic interactions produced by combining otherwise safe parent trees.

Guard (g) should apply to every merge commit, not just conflict merges. The resulting merge tree should be compared with both parents, scanned, and subjected to repository-specific validation. Ideally, the charter should also require that the exact approved merge SHA and tree SHA remain unchanged between verification and push.

2. Removing (i), the default-deny scope and term boundary, creates the greatest governance risk. Without it, a narrowly delegated operation can quietly become a reusable authority, potentially reaching the governance repo or monorepo. If scope enforcement exists independently, then (f)—post-push verification and global stop—is the most important operational guard.

Guard (d), one ledger line per push, is closest to theatre unless the ledger is immutable, independently stored, and records at least donor, remote URL, previous remote SHA, pushed SHA/tree, scan evidence, actor credential, timestamp, and verification result. A line written by the same agent into mutable local storage provides little control.

Guard (b) is also weaker than its wording suggests: useful as an ancestry constraint, but not a content-integrity control.

3. The likely uncovered failure is a side effect of publishing `main`, especially CI/CD or automation triggered by the push. A merge may introduce or activate workflows, deployments, package publication, infrastructure changes, or automation with access to repository secrets. A secret scan says nothing about those effects.

Other probable gaps include:

- TOCTOU: remote `main`, the local merge, hooks, configuration, or scan inputs change between approval and push.
- Pushing to the wrong repository because the remote URL is misconfigured or replaced.
- Remote branch rules allowing an unexpected rewrite or another actor updating `main` immediately after verification.
- Missing LFS objects or inaccessible submodule commits.
- Server-side hooks accepting the ref but failing downstream operations.
- The factory credential having broader repository or administrative rights than this charter intends.

Require an allowlisted, immutable remote URL and repository identity; a pre-push `ls-remote`; an atomic compare-and-swap/force-with-lease-style expectation without permitting rewrites; verification of the local SHA/tree immediately before pushing; and an assessment of push-triggered automation.

4. Serial publication with automatic freeze is appropriate as a containment mechanism, but freeze release must itself be controlled. Otherwise it can create operational pressure to “clear the queue” without resolving the cause.

A sound release should require a human to record:

- The diagnosed cause.
- Whether any remote state changed.
- The remediation and new evidence.
- Whether the failed donor is retried, skipped, or removed.
- Explicit authorization to resume the remaining queue.

This is stronger than requiring human approval for every donor while retaining most of the automation benefit. Per-donor human approval would reduce delegated risk further, but it also risks becoming routine click-through approval. A mandatory human checkpoint after the first successful donor would be prudent because it validates the charter and mechanics before the other nine pushes.

5. Rotation is sound cleanup, but it is not the primary security boundary. It only terminates future use; it does not constrain what the credential can do during the iteration.

The credential should be:

- Dedicated to this operation.
- Limited to the ten named repositories and branch-write only.
- Unable to administer repositories, alter protections, manage secrets, or access excluded repositories.
- Short-lived where possible.
- Auditable independently of the factory.
- Revoked immediately on freeze, compromise, or term expiry—not merely after normal completion.

Unsafe to approve as written: guard (g) applies only to conflict merges; there is no explicit protection against clean “evil merges,” no pre-push remote compare-and-swap requirement, no remote-identity pinning, and no treatment of CI/CD side effects. Credential rotation does not compensate for those omissions.
